## 1. The security shopping list is not yet a shopping bag

*India · Defence, public spending & industrial policy*

India’s Defence Acquisition Council granted in-principle approval on Monday to military proposals estimated at ₹2.12 lakh crore, about $22 billion. The list includes helicopters, artillery, air-defence and counter-drone systems, surveillance equipment, long-range drones and naval platforms; the government says approximately 82% of the proposed value would be sourced from Indian industry.

Acceptance of Necessity is an administrative beginning, not a signed stack of contracts. That distinction matters. The announcement describes both a security plan and an industrial policy: warfare is becoming more unmanned, networked and defensive, while orders are expected to deepen domestic manufacturing. Later negotiations will decide prices, suppliers, delivery schedules and how much of the headline survives procurement.

A serious country must prepare for danger; a serious democracy must also make the preparation auditable. Weapons are purchased with money that cannot be spent twice. The question is whether necessity has been demonstrated, competition is honest and each rupee buys the protection claimed for it.

What evidence should turn an in-principle military need into an irreversible public expense?

## 2. The students won; now comes the syllabus nobody supplied

*Serbia · Democracy, protest & governing competence*

Serbia’s election commission said preliminary results from 8,187 polling stations gave the Student List 52.71% and 140 seats, against 39.17% and 104 seats for Aleksandar Vučić’s United Serbia list. Vučić conceded on Sunday, ending the political dominance he had exercised as president or prime minister since 2014.

The movement grew after sixteen people died when a railway-station canopy collapsed in Novi Sad in 2024, turning demands for accountability into a coalition of students, professors, farmers and other candidates. Its leaderless structure frustrated attempts to discredit one figure. It also leaves a practical question: the qualities that protect a protest can complicate cabinet-making, discipline and compromise.

Belgrade’s mood was ebullient, and deservedly so. Yet removing entrenched power is the clean sentence; governing is the paragraph full of commas. Democratic courage now has to become budgets, appointments, independent institutions and restraint toward yesterday’s opponents. Can a movement organised to resist power learn to exercise it without becoming what it opposed?

## 3. A nurse was killed by the rumour he came to defeat

*Democratic Republic of the Congo · Public health, misinformation & trust*

Youths armed with machetes and batons attacked two Ebola-outreach workers in Bafwasende on Friday, killing an experienced local nurse and injuring a teacher. Witness accounts said the attackers accused them of contaminating water. Other health workers subsequently sheltered at the hospital under police protection.

This was not merely a shortage of correct information. WHO’s own reporting describes families frightened by claims that treatment centres administer lethal injections. In places marked by conflict, opaque authority and earlier failures, a rumour can take root because it gives fear a person to blame. Correction requires facts delivered through trusted relationships, alongside treatment people can see is humane.

The nurse was not a convenient symbol; he was a neighbour doing dangerous work, and he is dead. His killing also harms patients who lose both a caregiver and the colleagues now afraid to go home. Truth here is not an ornament of public debate. It is protective equipment.

**A question to carry**

Who can speak credibly when fear has made the healer look like the threat?

## 4. The chips are booming; the shopping cart is not

*South Korea · Growth, exports & household life*

Advance Bank of Korea estimates show the economy grew 1.0% in the third quarter from the previous quarter, beating a 0.7% consensus forecast. Exports rose 2.3% and facilities investment 4.1%, led by semiconductor demand and manufacturing equipment. Private consumption fell 0.2%, its first decline in nearly four years.

This is real growth with a narrow engine. Global spending on computing infrastructure has made South Korea’s chipmakers extraordinarily valuable, but export records do not automatically become confidence in the supermarket aisle. High borrowing costs, household debt and a softer labour market can coexist with factories running hard for overseas customers.

National averages are truthful and incomplete. They can describe a country whose output expands while many households feel they are contracting. The task is not to belittle an export achievement; it is to ask whether its wages, suppliers, taxes and investment spread resilience beyond one dazzling industry.

**A question to carry**

When does a semiconductor boom become a household recovery?

## 5. Climate science is stalled between the clock and the room

*Global / Ethiopia · Climate science, representation & institutions*

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change suspended its Addis Ababa session without agreeing on the timetable for three major Seventh Assessment Report volumes or the supporting budget. Some governments want reports ready for the 2028 global climate stocktake; others, including India and several fossil-fuel-reliant or developing states, argue that later publication would permit fuller participation and review.

The strongest arguments on both sides deserve respect. Evidence should reach policy before crucial decisions, and climate science should not become a wealthy-country conversation translated for everyone else. But tying the budget to an unresolved calendar now threatens the meetings and staff that would improve both timeliness and representation.

The paradox is almost bureaucratically perfect: governments are delaying the machinery needed to decide how not to rush. Inclusion and urgency are being presented as rivals when a capable institution should finance both. The atmosphere will not pause while the minutes are corrected.

**A question to carry**

What compromise protects scientific breadth without making relevance arrive one summit late?

## 6. The forest kept a five-pound secret

*China · Science, forests & wonder*

Researchers have formally described Dianjunus rex, collected in Yunnan’s forests, as a new species, genus and fungal family. Its upright fruiting structures can reach 50 centimetres and 2.2 kilograms—the largest documented in the vast Ascomycota group. DNA and anatomical evidence place it in the order Xylariales.

A molecular-clock estimate puts the lineage’s divergence at roughly 65 million years ago, with a wide 50–80 million-year range. That does not mean this individual fungus watched dinosaurs depart; it means its branch of the family tree may have arisen amid the ecological reorganisation after the mass extinction. The reason for its extravagant size remains unsettled.

The name D-rex is science allowing itself one decent joke. The deeper pleasure is epistemic humility: a half-metre organism can stand in a studied forest while science lacks even the family name for it. Creation remains lavish where our catalogues remain thin.

**A question to carry**

What becomes visible when wonder is disciplined enough to measure, sequence and revise?
