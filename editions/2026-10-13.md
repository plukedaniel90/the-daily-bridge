# The Daily Bridge — 13 October 2026

Edition 23 · Daily · 7 min read

Today the world keeps asking us to distinguish the thing from its announcement: prices from percentages, peace from the word, investment from excitement, a settlement from the state that should follow it, a model from the people inside it, and a painting from the empty space on the wall.

## 1. The index reaches the kitchen

*India · Inflation and household life*

India's annual consumer inflation climbed to 5.54% in September, up from 5.08% in August and above the Reserve Bank's 4% target for a fourth month. Food and energy did much of the lifting. Economists now see a stronger case for another interest-rate rise, though higher rates cool demand; they do not persuade a tomato plant to hurry or an oil tanker to become cheaper.

A national percentage is neat. A household budget is not. Inflation is regressive in experience even when the statistic is impartial: a wealthier family postpones a purchase, while a poorer one quietly removes protein, medicine or a bus journey. We should resist both easy stories—that price rises are merely government failure, or merely weather and therefore nobody's responsibility. Policy cannot command the monsoon, but it can decide who receives protection when scarcity arrives. The index measures the fever; justice asks who is shivering.

Whose breakfast changes first when the national average rises?

## 2. A ceasefire made chiefly of sentences

*Ukraine and Russia · War, diplomacy and truth*

Ukrainian officials said Russian attacks struck a power plant in Kharkiv, a gas facility and other energy sites on Monday, hours after Donald Trump announced that Moscow and Kyiv had agreed to stop attacking energy infrastructure. Ukraine said it had refrained from such strikes. Russia's position, as reported by the Associated Press, was less clear, and the competing claims had not been independently resolved. Meanwhile, civilians still had to ask the blunt question: will the lights stay on tonight?

A declaration can open a door; diplomacy often begins before trust does. But announcement is not accomplishment. When power rewards the appearance of progress, language can produce a simulacrum—a likeness of peace with none of peace's shelter. Scripture treats truthful speech as covenantal, not cosmetic: a yes should become yes in the world, not merely at a microphone. The point is not cynicism. It is to honour peace enough to verify it, because those beneath the missiles cannot live inside a press release.

What evidence should citizens demand before a promised peace is treated as a fact?

## 3. Nigeria buys a piece of the refinery dream

*Nigeria · Markets and public participation*

Dangote Industries has launched a $1.6 billion public offering in its vast Nigerian oil refinery, deliberately courting ordinary Nigerians as well as institutions. Trading platforms strained under the attention; social media supplied its customary financial instrument, the meme. Fewer than 5% of Nigerian adults currently invest in shares, so a successful sale could widen participation and give African capital markets unusual depth. Some analysts, however, question the valuation.

There is a generous story here: citizens should be able to own part of the infrastructure that shapes their economy, not merely buy its fuel. There is also a sales story, polished to a patriotic shine. To put your money where your mouth is means acting on belief; it does not mean switching off arithmetic. Capital markets become genuinely public when access, understandable information and accountability grow together. Otherwise inclusion may simply mean that risk has found more addresses. The market's oldest magic trick is making enthusiasm look like evidence.

When does national pride become an investment thesis—and when should it not?

## 4. After the treaty, the empty road

*Colombia · Peace, territory and the state*

A car bomb exploded at a toll station in Colombia's Cauca department on Monday, injuring at least three civilians. Over the weekend, two soldiers were killed in separate attacks blamed on a FARC dissident faction and the ELN. Colombia's 2016 accord disarmed roughly 14,000 FARC members and ended one immense chapter of war. It did not end the competition for remote territory, illicit routes and mining income; in places where the state arrived slowly, smaller armed groups moved in quickly.

This is not evidence that peacemaking was foolish. It is evidence that signing peace and governing peace are different crafts. A vacuum has no moral preference; whoever can command it will. Isaiah 32:17 imagines peace as the fruit of righteousness—of order made just—not as silence purchased for an afternoon. For rural families, peace must acquire roads, schools, courts, livelihoods and officials who remain after the cameras leave. Otherwise the treaty is historic while fear stays local.

What must arrive after the guns leave for peace to become ordinary life?

## 5. The economy discovers that people are inside it

*Sweden and the global economy · Economics and human behaviour*

Robert Hall and Michael Woodford have received the 2026 economics prize for strengthening the microeconomic foundations of stabilisation policy. In plainer English: they helped economists explain big movements—output, inflation, unemployment—through the choices and expectations of households and firms. Their work shaped thinking about interest rates, forward guidance and why prices do not instantly adjust. Economists have been honoured for proving that the economy contains people, a finding long suspected at kitchen tables.

The achievement is real. Governments must act, and disciplined models are better than intuition dressed in a tie. Yet models can become ideologies when their useful simplifications are mistaken for the whole person. People anticipate policy, but they also fear, imitate, trust, panic, sacrifice and sometimes behave magnificently against incentive. The biblical view of humanity is similarly resistant to reduction: neither angel nor calculator, but image-bearer and sinner, neighbour and mystery. A sound model should make power humbler, not give it the illusion of omniscience.

When does a model illuminate human behaviour, and when does its elegance hide what it cannot know?

## 6. Two Renoirs come out from behind the wall

*France · Art, crime and recovery*

French investigators have recovered two Renoir paintings stolen on September 8 from the artist's former home in Cagnes-sur-Mer. DNA found on a protective cover and the tracking of vehicles led police to the works, hidden behind a wall. Four men face preliminary charges. The paintings, together valued at about €4.2 million, suffered a torn canvas and frame damage, but conservators believe they can be repaired. Investigators say the arrests may also have prevented another theft.

The caper had the confidence of cinema and the housekeeping of real life: somebody left DNA on the cover. Yet the happier fact is recovery. A painting is not useful in the way a bridge or vaccine is useful; that is part of its gift. It trains attention, carries memory and makes a room larger than its walls. Not every headline needs to be converted into a warning. Today, two wounded works of beauty are coming home, and careful hands will begin the patient work of repair.

What do we learn about value from the trouble people take both to steal beauty and to bring it home?

## A little language to take away

- **Simulacrum** (sim-yuh-LAY-krum): A likeness or representation of something, especially one that may lack the substance of the original. *The declaration produced a simulacrum of peace: its vocabulary without its shelter.* [Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/simulacrum)
- **Put your money where your mouth is**: To show by your actions, especially by spending or risking money, that you truly support what you say. *Investors put their money where their mouth was, though conviction still needed arithmetic.* [Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/put-money-where-mouth-is)
