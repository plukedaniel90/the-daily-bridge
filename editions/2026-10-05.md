# The Daily Bridge — 5 October 2026

Edition 15 · Daily · 7 min read

Today, nations keep offering large words—strength, liberation, growth, success. The useful work is to ask what those words mean on a household budget, in a captured city, at an empty stadium, and beneath the wide Rajasthan sky.

## 1. India grows quickly; the interest-rate door is still closing

*India · Economy*

India's Monetary Policy Committee begins a three-day meeting today. Thirty-five of 61 economists polled by Reuters expect the repo rate to rise by a quarter point, to 5.50%. Inflation reached 4.82% in August, above the RBI's 4% target for a third month, while prices in nearly half the inflation basket were rising by at least 4%.

The usual story says a fast-growing economy is a healthy one. India did grow nearly 8% in the April–June quarter. Yet that strength gives the RBI room to make borrowing dearer. The repo rate sounds like plumbing until it reaches a home loan, a small factory or a shopkeeper's working capital. Inflation punishes cash quietly; higher interest rates announce themselves in the monthly instalment. Wise policy must choose between harms. The biblical demand for honest weights begins there: tell the truth about the measure, and about the people hidden inside its average.

Whose household bears the medicine prescribed for the national average?

## 2. Brazil chose a runoff—and surprised the pollsters

*Brazil · Politics*

Brazil will vote again on October 25. With almost all ballots counted, Senator Flávio Bolsonaro took about 47% in Sunday's first round; President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva received 44.9%. Surveys had shown Lula ahead. The ballot box, which enjoys correcting confident people, supplied its own footnote.

The campaign turned on the cost of living, corruption and fear about public safety. Unemployment and inequality have improved, yet daily life can still feel narrower. Bolsonaro also carries a formidable surname—his father, former president Jair Bolsonaro, is imprisoned for plotting a coup after the 2022 election. A democracy may inherit political families; it must not inherit their entitlement. Psalm 146:3 refuses ultimate trust in princes, not because public power is irrelevant, but because no ruler can bear messianic weight. Brazil now gets three weeks to ask for policy beneath the names.

What grievance did the polls hear poorly because the averages sounded better?

## 3. Mekelle changed hands; peace did not return with it

*Ethiopia · Conflict*

Ethiopian government forces and allied militias took Mekelle, capital of Tigray, on Sunday. The Tigray People's Liberation Front said it had withdrawn and would move the regional government elsewhere. The loss is a severe blow to the alliance that began an offensive on September 23, in the gravest fighting since Ethiopia's 2020–2022 civil war.

A capital can be captured faster than consent. The counteroffensive changes who occupies roads and buildings; it does not resolve Tigray's political future, contested borders or the fear pulling neighbours toward the conflict. Ethiopia accuses Eritrea of aiding the rebels; Eritrea, Sudan and Egypt deny support. The word victory is premature. Scripture's shalom is not merely silence after gunfire but life put right—neighbours secure, truth spoken, wrongs answered. Mekelle's residents need more than a new flag over an old uncertainty.

What would make the people of Tigray safer after the headlines move on?

## 4. Another campaign promises to finish Yemen's unfinished war

*Yemen · War and diplomacy*

Yemen's Saudi-backed, internationally recognised government announced on Sunday a campaign to recapture all Houthi-held territory. The movement has controlled Sanaa and much of the populous northwest since 2014; last month it seized the Bab el-Mandeb strait and Red Sea coast. The Houthis also claimed missile and drone strikes on Saudi oil sites, though Saudi Arabia had not confirmed them.

The government's chosen word is liberation. It is a serious claim: Houthi rule is coercive and the shipping route matters far beyond Yemen. But 'liberate everything' is also the sort of sentence maps permit generals to say. Maps do not show the child under the roof, the nurse beside a failing generator or the family displaced twice. After years of failed interventions, military momentum needs a believable political destination. The biblical vision of power never allows rulers to turn image-bearers into background. Before accepting liberation, ask what life the liberated are expected to inherit.

What evidence would show that this offensive can produce a political order rather than another round of ruin?

## 5. The teenagers were ready; the buses were less certain

*Japan and Asia · Sport and public life*

The Asian Games closed in Aichi–Nagoya after assembling about 17,000 athletes and officials—more than the Paris Olympics—for 469 medal events. Organisers promised a 'simple, streamlined and functional' Games. With no central village and widely scattered venues, accommodation and transport failed, apologies multiplied, and stands remained conspicuously empty. Bureaucracy, unlike sprinting, rarely knows when the gun has gone off.

Yet the competition shimmered. China's 13-year-old swimmer Yu Zidi won three individual golds; 17-year-old Shin Ohashi of Japan broke the 200-metre breaststroke world record. India finished fourth overall. This is not an excuse for poor organisation. It is a sharper distinction: administrators can make excellence harder without extinguishing it. The Games' vicissitudes exposed an old habit—institutions borrowing glory from people who succeeded despite them. Frugality is honourable; hospitality still requires the bus to arrive.

When does simplicity become wisdom, and when is it merely under-preparation with a good adjective?

## 6. Two great bustards walked out of captivity and into possibility

*Rajasthan, India · Conservation*

Two captive-bred male great Indian bustards were released into Rajasthan's Desert National Park on Sunday—the first such rewilding in India. The programme collected wild eggs, incubated them and bred the species in captivity. Ninety-eight captive-bred bustards now live at the centres; 11 are selected for release. Only about 150 remain in the wild.

This is progress, and still a beginning. Captive breeding can preserve a lineage; it cannot manufacture safe grassland or remove every power line from a flight path. The proof of the pudding is in the eating: survival and reproduction in the wild will test the programme. The bird also possesses what philosophers call haecceity—its irreducible thisness. It is not merely 'biodiversity', a tourism asset or a number saved from zero. Psalm 104 sees creatures within fitting habitats, belonging to a creation that exceeds human usefulness. Conservation begins when wonder acquires duties.

Can development learn to leave enough room for a creature whose worth cannot be priced?

## A little language to take away

- **Vicissitude** (vuh-SISS-ih-tood): An unexpected change of circumstances or fortune, often a difficult one. *A durable peace must survive the vicissitudes that follow the signing ceremony.* [Dictionary](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/vicissitude) · [Pronunciation](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/pronunciation/english/vicissitude)
- **Haecceity** (heck-SEE-uh-tee): The quality that makes a person or thing uniquely this particular person or thing—its 'thisness'. *The old banyan had a haecceity no landscape plan could replace.* [Dictionary](https://www.collinsdictionary.com/dictionary/english/haecceity) · [Pronunciation](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/pronunciation/english/haecceity)
- **The proof of the pudding is in the eating**: You can judge the quality of something only after it has been tried or experienced. *The pilot scheme looked excellent on paper; the proof of the pudding was in the eating.* [Source](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/proof-of-the-pudding-is-in-the-eating)
