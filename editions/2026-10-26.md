## 1. The comeback won; the governing begins in a headwind

*Brazil · Elections, democracy & governing power*

Brazil’s electoral court declared Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva the winner of Sunday’s presidential runoff. With all votes counted, he received 51.8%, leading Senator Flávio Bolsonaro by nearly 4.4 million votes. Lula had finished behind Bolsonaro in the first round; his new term begins on 5 January 2027.

The result is a remarkable personal return and a narrow national instruction. Lula overcame cost-of-living concerns, security anxieties and a regional turn toward the right, but victory did not dissolve them. A conservative Congress can obstruct his programme, while controversy over a last-minute extension of voting hours may keep partisans arguing about procedure after the count is complete.

An election can choose a ruler without reconciling a people. The biblical suspicion of triumphal power is useful here: authority is judged by what it does for those outside the celebration. A mandate becomes mature when it is treated as stewardship rather than vindication. Can a dramatic comeback be governed as repair rather than revenge?

## 2. The governing majority says no to its government’s arithmetic

*Senegal · Debt, budgets & democratic accountability*

Senegal’s Pastef party, which holds a large National Assembly majority, said on Sunday that it would not pass the government’s revised budget. It cited disputed spending choices, financing arrangements and inadequate guarantees to parliament, while remaining open to negotiations.

The refusal could demonstrate scrutiny inside a governing movement; it could also delay a $2.2 billion IMF programme supporting debt restructuring. Both possibilities matter. Senegal discovered billions in previously misreported borrowing, and IMF figures put public debt at 132% of GDP by the end of 2024. The amended budget cuts forecast growth to 2.7% and raises the deficit estimate to 7.6%, partly because energy subsidies have tripled.

Hidden debt does not disappear; it changes the identity of the person who pays. Transparency belongs to economic justice because future taxpayers cannot question decisions concealed from present voters. Yet scrutiny must eventually produce a budget. Refusing bad arithmetic is only the beginning of better arithmetic.

How can parliament demand honesty without turning delay into another public cost?

## 3. Foreign money leaves; the market is not the country

*India · Markets, capital & economic confidence*

Depository data show foreign portfolio investors withdrew a net ₹48,056 crore from Indian equities in October through the 25th. Net equity outflows for 2026 reached ₹3.08 lakh crore, exceeding the ₹1.66 lakh crore withdrawn during all of 2025. Selling eased late in the week, with foreigners becoming net buyers on two days.

The departure reflects a mixture of high crude prices, West Asian conflict, earnings worries and attractive yields elsewhere. Capital crosses borders by comparing returns, currencies and risk; it is neither a patriotic vote nor an infallible judgment on India. Domestic investment has cushioned the market, and overseas interest in new share offerings suggests some money is being rearranged rather than simply abandoned.

Still, movable money has power over people who cannot move. Sustained outflows can pressure the rupee and financing conditions long before a distant fund manager learns the price of coriander. The wise reading avoids panic and swagger: a market signal is partial, but partial signals can become expensive when ignored.

## 4. Forty sailors and the narrow bridge of mercy

*Sri Lanka & Iran · Maritime rescue, sanctions & human dignity*

Sri Lanka coordinated medical assistance for 40 Iranian sailors from 19 commercial ships near its waters, brought them to Galle and supported their return to Iran early Sunday. Officials described unspecified medical emergencies. The ships had reportedly faced shortages after being stranded amid the United States’ blockade of Iranian shipping.

Sri Lanka is recovering from its own economic crisis and depends on relationships with both Washington and Tehran. Helping the sailors required separating humanitarian duty from endorsement of a government or cargo. Sanctions aim at state behaviour, yet their pressure travels through bodies that become thirsty, ill and frightened.

This was public solicitude—care made administrative through harbour access, treatment, immigration papers and transport home. Any port in a storm may sound like resignation; for a sick sailor it can mean survival. The humane state preserves a narrow bridge on which an enemy, stranger or sanctioned crew member can remain a person.

**A question to carry**

Can humanitarian exceptions remain credible when geopolitics rewards suspicion of every exception?

## 5. The airport is open; the city is still travelling toward it

*Australia · Infrastructure, cities & ordinary travel*

Western Sydney International Airport opened to passengers on Sunday. The A$5.6 billion airport at Badgerys Creek sits about 60 kilometres west of central Sydney and can operate around the clock, unlike the city’s older airport. Authorities say its eventual capacity exceeds 80 million passenger movements a year.

The strongest case for the project is regional: western Sydney gains jobs, freight capacity and direct international access after decades of population growth. Its harder test is connection. Roads are being upgraded and a Metro link is planned; until the surrounding network matures, a splendid terminal can still begin with an inelegant journey to the terminal.

Large infrastructure is built twice: first in concrete, then in the habits of people who decide whether it belongs to their lives. Opening day celebrates completion. The next decade will reveal distribution—whose commute shortens, whose neighbourhood bears noise, and whether promised opportunity lands beyond the runway.

**A question to carry**

When should a megaproject be judged: at the ribbon, or after ordinary people have rearranged their lives around it?

## 6. Science rediscovered what local rescuers never lost

*Indonesia · Wildlife, knowledge & rediscovery*

A new study confirms that the Bangka slow loris survives across much of Indonesia’s Bangka Island. The last historical scientific records dated from the early 1930s, and the IUCN had classified the species as critically endangered and possibly extinct. Yet the local Alobi rescue centre documented more than 90 animals between 2015 and 2025 and released 86 after rehabilitation.

The animal was lost to scientific literature, not to local sight. That distinction restores proportion to the word discovery. Formal research brought scattered records into publishable evidence; community rescuers supplied the encounters, care and memory without which there would have been nothing to publish.

Good news must keep its warning label. Illegal tin mining and clearing for oil palm have greatly reduced the loris’s habitat. Being found is not the same as being safe. Still, there is beauty here: a slow, large-eyed, venomous primate continued moving through the trees while the official record waited nearly a century to catch up.

**A question to carry**

What knowledge is missing because institutions have not yet learned whom to ask?

## A little language to take away

- **Solicitude** (suh-LIS-ih-tood): Formal: care or concern for another person’s comfort, health or safety. *The nurse’s quiet solicitude steadied the frightened traveller.* [Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/solicitude) · [Pronunciation](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/pronunciation/english/solicitude)
- **Any port in a storm**: In a very difficult situation, any available source of help may be welcome, even if it is not ideal. *The hotel was hardly charming, but after the cancelled night train it was any port in a storm.* [Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/dictionary/english/any-port-in-a-storm)
